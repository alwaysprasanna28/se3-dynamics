# Mathematical and Mechanical Foundations

> [!summary]
> Robotics is easiest when we distinguish a geometric object from the coordinates used to describe it. This note builds that habit and reviews the linear algebra, calculus, mechanics, and configuration-space ideas required later.

## 1. Physical quantities and coordinates

A geometric vector such as a displacement exists independently of coordinates. Once a basis $\{e_1,e_2,e_3\}$ is chosen, the vector gets a coordinate column:

$$
v=v_1e_1+v_2e_2+v_3e_3
\quad\longleftrightarrow\quad
[v]_{\mathcal B}=
\begin{bmatrix}v_1\\v_2\\v_3\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The vector is not the column; the column is its representation in basis $\mathcal B$. If the same physical vector is represented in two orthonormal frames $a$ and $b$, then

$$
v_a=R_{ab}v_b.
$$

$R_{ab}$ changes coordinates from frame $b$ to frame $a$. This frame-aware reading will later generalize to points, twists, wrenches, and inertias.

> [!warning] The most common source of robotics errors
> A formula can be dimensionally correct and still be geometrically meaningless if its terms are expressed in different frames or taken about different points.

## 2. Linear maps

A matrix $A\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}$ represents a linear map from $\mathbb R^n$ to $\mathbb R^m$ after bases are chosen. Important spaces are:

- the range $\mathcal R(A)$, all outputs attainable as $Ax$;
- the nullspace $\mathcal N(A)$, all $x$ satisfying $Ax=0$;
- the row space $\mathcal R(A^T)$;
- the left nullspace $\mathcal N(A^T)$.

The rank-nullity theorem says

$$
\operatorname{rank}(A)+\operatorname{nullity}(A)=n.
$$

For a robot Jacobian $J\in\mathbb R^{6\times n}$:

- $\mathcal R(J)$ contains attainable end-effector twists;
- $\mathcal N(J)$ contains joint motions that instantaneously leave the task pose unchanged;
- $\mathcal R(J^T)$ contains joint torques generated by task wrenches;
- $\mathcal N(J^T)$ contains task wrenches that create no joint torque.

### 2.1 Inner products, norms, and positive definiteness

The Euclidean inner product and norm are

$$
\langle x,y\rangle=x^Ty,
\qquad
\|x\|_2=\sqrt{x^Tx}.
$$

A symmetric matrix $A=A^T$ is positive definite, written $A\succ0$, if

$$
x^TAx>0\quad\text{for every }x\ne0.
$$

Then $\|x\|_A=\sqrt{x^TAx}$ is a weighted norm. Robot inertia matrices are symmetric positive definite for independent generalized coordinates because kinetic energy must be positive for every nonzero generalized velocity.

### 2.2 Eigenvalues and principal directions

For symmetric $A$, the spectral theorem gives

$$
A=Q\Lambda Q^T,
$$

where $Q$ is orthogonal and $\Lambda$ is real diagonal. For a rotational inertia tensor, eigenvectors are principal axes and eigenvalues are principal moments. For a task-space inertia, eigenvectors and eigenvalues describe directions of dynamically easy and hard motion.

### 2.3 The pseudoinverse

For a possibly rectangular matrix $J$, the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse $J^+$ produces the minimum-norm least-squares solution. If $J$ has full row rank,

$$
J^+=J^T(JJ^T)^{-1}.
$$

If $J$ has full column rank,

$$
J^+=(J^TJ)^{-1}J^T.
$$

Near a singularity, a damped least-squares inverse is better conditioned:

$$
J_\lambda^+=J^T(JJ^T+\lambda^2I)^{-1}.
$$

This sacrifices exact inversion for bounded joint velocities.

## 3. The cross product as a matrix

For $a=[a_1,a_2,a_3]^T$, define

$$
[a]_\times=a^\wedge=
\begin{bmatrix}
0&-a_3&a_2\\
a_3&0&-a_1\\
-a_2&a_1&0
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Then

$$
a\times b=[a]_\times b.
$$

Useful identities are

$$
[a]_\times^T=-[a]_\times,
$$

$$
[Ra]_\times=R[a]_\times R^T\quad\text{for }R\in SO(3),
$$

$$
[a\times b]_\times=[a]_\times[b]_\times-[b]_\times[a]_\times,
$$

$$
[a]_\times^2=aa^T-\|a\|^2I.
$$

The inverse operation is the vee map:

$$
([a]_\times)^\vee=a.
$$

## 4. Differentiation of vectors and matrices

### 4.1 Product and inverse rules

For time-varying matrices,

$$
\frac{d}{dt}(AB)=\dot AB+A\dot B.
$$

From $A^{-1}A=I$,

$$
\frac{d}{dt}A^{-1}=-A^{-1}\dot A A^{-1}.
$$

For a scalar function $f(q)$, the gradient is the column satisfying

$$
\delta f=\nabla_qf^T\delta q.
$$

For a vector function $y=f(q)$, the Jacobian is

$$
J(q)=\frac{\partial f}{\partial q},
\qquad
\dot y=J(q)\dot q.
$$

Differentiating again gives

$$
\ddot y=J(q)\ddot q+\dot J(q,\dot q)\dot q.
$$

The second term is the bias acceleration caused by moving through a configuration-dependent map.

### 4.2 A key derivative for rotations

If $R(t)^TR(t)=I$, differentiation gives

$$
\dot R^TR+R^T\dot R=0.
$$

Therefore $R^T\dot R$ is skew-symmetric. Every skew-symmetric $3\times3$ matrix corresponds to one angular-velocity vector. This observation is the bridge from $SO(3)$ to its Lie algebra $\mathfrak{so}(3)$.

## 5. From Euclidean spaces to manifolds

A smooth manifold is a space that looks locally Euclidean even if its global geometry is not a Euclidean vector space. A sphere is locally two-dimensional but cannot be represented globally by one nonsingular pair of coordinates. Likewise, $SO(3)$ is locally three-dimensional, but no global three-parameter orientation chart avoids singularity or discontinuity.

At each configuration $q$ on a manifold $\mathcal Q$ is a tangent space $T_q\mathcal Q$. A velocity belongs to that tangent space:

$$
\dot q\in T_q\mathcal Q.
$$

Forces naturally belong to the dual space $T_q^*\mathcal Q$, whose elements are covectors. Pairing a generalized force $\tau$ with a velocity gives power:

$$
P=\langle\tau,\dot q\rangle=\tau^T\dot q.
$$

This duality explains why velocities and forces transform differently.

### 5.1 Configuration spaces of common joints

- Prismatic joint: $q_i\in\mathbb R$.
- Continuous revolute joint: $q_i\in S^1$.
- Spherical joint: $R_i\in SO(3)$.
- Free rigid body: $T_i\in SE(3)$.
- Fixed-base $n$-revolute serial arm: locally $q\in\mathbb R^n$, globally often $\mathcal Q=(S^1)^n$ subject to joint limits.

Thus, an arm is not generally “an element of $SE(3)$.” Its individual link poses are elements of $SE(3)$, and its end-effector pose is the output of a map into $SE(3)$.

## 6. Newtonian mechanics from first principles

### 6.1 Particle mechanics

For mass $m$, position $p$, velocity $v=\dot p$, and acceleration $a=\dot v$,

$$
f=ma.
$$

Linear momentum and kinetic energy are

$$
\ell=mv,
\qquad
K=\frac12mv^Tv.
$$

Power is $P=f^Tv$. Since

$$
\dot K=mv^T\dot v=f^Tv,
$$

the rate of kinetic energy equals applied power.

### 6.2 Rotation about the center of mass

Let $I_C$ be the rotational inertia about the center of mass, expressed in a body-fixed frame, and let $\omega$ be angular velocity in that frame. Then

$$
K_{\mathrm{rot}}=\frac12\omega^TI_C\omega.
$$

The body-coordinate Euler equation is

$$
m_C=I_C\dot\omega+\omega\times(I_C\omega),
$$

where $m_C$ is the applied moment about the center of mass. The cross term is not an extra physical force; it appears because the coordinate frame itself rotates.

### 6.3 Work, potential, and conservative forces

For generalized coordinates $q$, a generalized force $\tau$ performs virtual work

$$
\delta W=\tau^T\delta q.
$$

If a force is conservative, there is a potential $U(q)$ such that

$$
\tau_{\mathrm{cons}}=-\frac{\partial U}{\partial q}.
$$

For gravity acceleration vector $g_0$, the gravitational potential of point masses is

$$
U(q)=-\sum_i m_i g_0^Tp_{C_i}(q).
$$

The conventional gravity term placed on the left of the manipulator equation is

$$
g(q)=\frac{\partial U}{\partial q}.
$$

### 6.4 Generalized coordinates and Lagrange's equation

Define the Lagrangian

$$
L(q,\dot q)=K(q,\dot q)-U(q).
$$

For generalized nonconservative force $\tau$, the Euler–Lagrange equation is

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_i}
-\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i}
=\tau_i.
$$

This is Newton's law expressed in arbitrary generalized coordinates. For a robot, it produces the manipulator equation studied in [[09 - Lagrangian Dynamics and the Two-Link Arm]].

## 7. Kinematics, statics, and dynamics

These three questions must not be mixed:

- **Kinematics:** Given $q,\dot q,\ddot q$, where are the bodies and how are they moving?
- **Statics:** When acceleration is zero, what forces and torques balance?
- **Dynamics:** Given the current state and applied forces, what acceleration results?

Inverse dynamics asks for torque given motion:

$$
(q,\dot q,\ddot q)\mapsto\tau.
$$

Forward dynamics asks for acceleration given torque:

$$
(q,\dot q,\tau)\mapsto\ddot q.
$$

## 8. Sanity checks for every derivation

1. **Dimensions:** Every added term must have identical units.
2. **Frame:** Every added vector must be expressed in the same frame.
3. **Point:** Moments, velocities, and inertias must refer to compatible points.
4. **Power:** $F^TV$ and $\tau^T\dot q$ must agree under coordinate maps.
5. **Energy:** With no input or dissipation, total mechanical energy should be conserved.
6. **Symmetry:** A physical inertia matrix should be symmetric.
7. **Positive definiteness:** Nonzero physical motion should have positive kinetic energy.
8. **Limiting cases:** Set masses, lengths, rates, or gravity to zero and inspect the result.
