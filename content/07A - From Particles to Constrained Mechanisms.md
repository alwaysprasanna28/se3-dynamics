# From Particles to Constrained Mechanisms

> [!summary]
> The manipulator equation is Newton's second law after rigid-joint constraints are encoded by generalized coordinates. The key operation is projection: allowable particle motion is generated by Jacobians, so physical forces and inertial forces are projected into joint space by Jacobian transposes.

## 1. What a dynamics model must predict

Kinematics answers where a system can move. Dynamics answers how its motion changes under applied forces. For a robot, the central forward-dynamics question is

$$
(q,\dot q,\tau,F_{\mathrm{ext}})
\longmapsto
\ddot q.
$$

The inverse-dynamics question reverses the last part:

$$
(q,\dot q,\ddot q,F_{\mathrm{ext}})
\longmapsto
\tau.
$$

We begin with particles because Newton's law is simplest there, then show how constraints and rigid bodies produce the standard robot equation.

## 2. An unconstrained collection of particles

Consider $N$ particles. Particle $a$ has mass $m_a$, position $r_a\in\mathbb R^3$, velocity $\dot r_a$, and applied force $f_a$. Newton's law is

$$
m_a\ddot r_a=f_a.
$$

Stacking all particle positions into

$$
r=\begin{bmatrix}r_1\\\vdots\\r_N\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb R^{3N}
$$

gives

$$
\mathsf M_p\ddot r=f,
$$

where

$$
\mathsf M_p=\operatorname{diag}(m_1I_3,\ldots,m_NI_3).
$$

This description has $3N$ position coordinates. A rigid mechanism has far fewer degrees of freedom because distances inside links and motions at joints are constrained.

## 3. Constraints and generalized coordinates

Holonomic constraints have the form

$$
\Phi(r)=0.
$$

For example, two particles connected by a rigid bar of length $l$ satisfy

$$
\|r_2-r_1\|^2-l^2=0.
$$

Rather than carrying every constraint force explicitly, choose independent generalized coordinates

$$
q=\begin{bmatrix}q_1&\cdots&q_n\end{bmatrix}^T
$$

that parameterize every admissible particle position:

$$
r_a=r_a(q).
$$

For a serial robot, the $q_i$ are normally joint displacements. The joint model has already encoded the rigid-link constraints. This is why a seven-joint arm can be modeled with seven configuration coordinates instead of millions of particle coordinates.

## 4. Velocity and acceleration of each particle

Differentiate $r_a(q)$:

$$
\dot r_a=J_a(q)\dot q,
$$

where

$$
J_a(q)=\frac{\partial r_a}{\partial q}
\in\mathbb R^{3\times n}.
$$

Differentiate again:

$$
\ddot r_a=J_a(q)\ddot q+\dot J_a(q,\dot q)\dot q.
$$

The first term is linear in generalized acceleration. The second exists because the allowable velocity directions change with configuration.

> [!example] Circular motion
> A point can have constant generalized speed and zero generalized acceleration while still having centripetal Cartesian acceleration. That acceleration is $\dot J_a\dot q$.

## 5. Virtual displacements

A virtual displacement is an infinitesimal displacement compatible with the constraints at one instant. Since $r_a=r_a(q)$,

$$
\delta r_a=J_a(q)\delta q.
$$

It is not a time evolution; $\delta$ compares nearby admissible configurations at fixed time.

The virtual work of particle forces is

$$
\delta W=\sum_a f_a^T\delta r_a.
$$

Substitute the virtual displacement:

$$
\delta W
=\sum_a f_a^TJ_a\delta q
=\left(\sum_aJ_a^Tf_a\right)^T\delta q.
$$

Therefore the generalized force is

$$
\boxed{
Q=\sum_aJ_a(q)^Tf_a
}.
$$

The transpose Jacobian is not a robotics trick. It is the unique pullback that preserves work and power.

## 6. Why ideal internal constraint forces disappear

A rigid bar develops tension or compression to maintain its length. A perfect revolute joint develops reaction forces that prevent forbidden translation. These forces can be large, but ideal constraint forces do no virtual work along admissible virtual displacements:

$$
\sum_a(f_a^{\mathrm{constraint}})^T\delta r_a=0.
$$

For a frictionless pin joint, the two connected links experience equal and opposite reaction forces at the same point. Their virtual-work contributions cancel. Joint torque along the permitted rotation is not a constraint reaction; it is an applied generalized force and remains in the equations.

This cancellation is the main benefit of generalized coordinates: unknown internal reactions need not appear in the minimal equations of motion.

## 7. D'Alembert's principle

Newton says $f_a-m_a\ddot r_a=0$. D'Alembert's principle projects this residual onto every admissible virtual displacement:

$$
\sum_a
\left(f_a-m_a\ddot r_a\right)^T\delta r_a=0.
$$

Insert $\delta r_a=J_a\delta q$:

$$
\delta q^T
\sum_aJ_a^T
\left(f_a-m_a\ddot r_a\right)=0.
$$

Because $\delta q$ is arbitrary,

$$
\sum_aJ_a^Tm_a\ddot r_a
=\sum_aJ_a^Tf_a.
$$

Now substitute particle acceleration:

$$
\sum_aJ_a^Tm_a
\left(J_a\ddot q+\dot J_a\dot q\right)
=Q.
$$

Group the terms:

$$
\boxed{
M(q)\ddot q+b(q,\dot q)=Q
},
$$

where

$$
\boxed{
M(q)=\sum_a m_aJ_a(q)^TJ_a(q)
}
$$

and

$$
\boxed{
b(q,\dot q)=\sum_a m_aJ_a(q)^T\dot J_a(q,\dot q)\dot q
}.
$$

This is already a manipulator equation for a system of point masses.

## 8. Reading the three pieces physically

In

$$
M(q)\ddot q+b(q,\dot q)=Q,
$$

- $M(q)\ddot q$ is the generalized inertial force caused directly by generalized acceleration;
- $b(q,\dot q)$ is the generalized force required to bend the current motion through changing velocity directions;
- $Q$ is the projection of applied physical forces onto allowable generalized motion.

The matrix $M$ is configuration dependent even though every particle mass is constant. Geometry changes how a unit joint rate moves the particles.

## 9. Example: a point-mass pendulum

Let a point mass $m$ be attached to a massless rod of length $l$. Measure angle $q$ from the $+x$ axis, with $y$ upward:

$$
r(q)=
\begin{bmatrix}
l\cos q\\
l\sin q
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The Jacobian is

$$
J(q)=
\begin{bmatrix}
-l\sin q\\
l\cos q
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Thus

$$
M(q)=mJ^TJ=ml^2.
$$

Also,

$$
\dot J\dot q=
\begin{bmatrix}
-l\cos q\,\dot q^2\\
-l\sin q\,\dot q^2
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The velocity bias projected along the allowed tangent is

$$
mJ^T\dot J\dot q=0.
$$

The centripetal acceleration is radial, while the generalized motion is tangential, so the rod constraint—not the actuator—supplies the centripetal force.

Gravity force is

$$
f_g=\begin{bmatrix}0\\-mg\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Its generalized force is

$$
Q_g=J^Tf_g=-mgl\cos q.
$$

With applied joint torque $\tau$,

$$
ml^2\ddot q=\tau-mgl\cos q,
$$

or

$$
\boxed{ml^2\ddot q+mgl\cos q=\tau}.
$$

The radial constraint force never appears in the minimal equation.

## 10. Lagrange's equation from the same construction

Particle kinetic energy is

$$
K=\frac12\sum_am_a\dot r_a^T\dot r_a
=\frac12\dot q^TM(q)\dot q.
$$

For conservative potential $U(q)$ and nonconservative generalized force $Q_{\mathrm{nc}}$, Euler–Lagrange gives

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial K}{\partial\dot q}
-\frac{\partial K}{\partial q}
+\frac{\partial U}{\partial q}
=Q_{\mathrm{nc}}.
$$

This is equivalent to D'Alembert's projected Newton equation. D'Alembert emphasizes force and admissible motion; Lagrange emphasizes scalar energy. Neither introduces new physics.

## 11. From particles to one rigid link

A rigid body can be viewed as infinitely many particles whose body coordinates are fixed. If a body-fixed point has coordinate $\rho$, its body-coordinate velocity is generated by the body twist:

$$
v(\rho)=v_O+\omega\times\rho.
$$

Integrating particle kinetic energy over the body gives

$$
K_i=\frac12V_i^T\mathcal G_iV_i,
$$

where $\mathcal G_i$ is the $6\times6$ spatial inertia. Thus spatial inertia is the compact result of integrating the point-mass construction over a rigid mass distribution.

For a mechanism,

$$
V_i=J_i(q)\dot q,
$$

so

$$
K_i=\frac12\dot q^TJ_i^T\mathcal G_iJ_i\dot q.
$$

Summing links replaces the particle formula by

$$
M(q)=\sum_iJ_i^T\mathcal G_iJ_i.
$$

The mathematics has not changed: particle velocity Jacobians have been compressed into rigid-body twist Jacobians and spatial inertias.

## 12. Generalized forces in a robot

Applied generalized force can contain several sources:

$$
Q=B(q)u+\tau_g+\tau_{\mathrm{ext}}-\tau_f.
$$

- $u$ is the actuator input.
- $B$ maps actuator effort into generalized force; $B=I$ for a fully actuated fixed-base arm.
- $\tau_g$ is the physical generalized load of gravity.
- $\tau_{\mathrm{ext}}=\sum_iJ_i^TF_i$ comes from environmental wrenches.
- $\tau_f$ models friction or other dissipative loads.

Defining $g=-\tau_g$ and writing the velocity bias as $c$ produces

$$
M(q)\ddot q+c(q,\dot q)+g(q)+\tau_f
=B(q)u+\tau_{\mathrm{ext}}.
$$

## 13. Minimal versus redundant coordinates

Minimal coordinates eliminate ideal constraint reactions but may be hard to construct for closed chains. Redundant coordinates retain explicit constraints:

$$
\Phi(q)=0,
$$

and introduce Lagrange multipliers:

$$
M(q)\ddot q+h(q,\dot q)
=B u+J_c(q)^T\lambda.
$$

$\lambda$ represents constraint forces. [[10A - Constraints Contacts and Underactuation]] develops this formulation.

## 14. First-principles derivation template

For a new mechanism:

1. choose independent generalized coordinates $q$;
2. express each mass point or rigid-link pose as a function of $q$;
3. differentiate to obtain Jacobians;
4. build kinetic energy and $M(q)$;
5. build potential energy and $g(q)$;
6. obtain velocity bias through D'Alembert, Euler–Lagrange, or Newton–Euler;
7. project external forces by Jacobian transpose;
8. state actuator mapping and friction;
9. verify energy, units, symmetry, and limiting cases.
